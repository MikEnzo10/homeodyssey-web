Age-appropriate chores: what can kids aged 5 to 10 do at home?

“What chores can a six-year-old do?” It’s one of those questions most of us ask the moment we realise we’re still the ones putting the toys away. The short answer: a lot more than we think.

First things first: this is a rough guide, not a list of what your child “should” be doing. Every child goes at their own pace. What matters is starting with something they can do on their own and do well, and building up bit by bit.

Chores for 5 and 6-year-olds

At this age, short, specific jobs work best: ones where they can see they’ve finished.

  • Put their toys away in their box or on the shelf.
  • Take their plate and cutlery to the sink.
  • Put the napkins and cutlery on the table.
  • Put dirty clothes in the laundry basket.
  • Feed the pet (with a grown-up nearby).
  • Get dressed on their own with clothes laid out.
  • Water a plant.

Not reading yet? Use pictures: a bed, a toothbrush, a school bag.

Chores for 7 and 8-year-olds

They can take charge of small, complete routines.

  • Make their bed (even if it’s not perfect).
  • Pack their school bag for the next day.
  • Lay and clear the table.
  • Pair socks and put their folded clothes away.
  • Help write the shopping list.
  • Tidy their room with a step-by-step list.
  • Have a shower with a little supervision.

Chores for 9 and 10-year-olds

They can take on jobs that help the whole family.

  • Make a simple breakfast or an after-school snack.
  • Take out the bins and the recycling.
  • Hoover their bedroom.
  • Put a wash on (with help the first time).
  • Help a younger brother or sister with their routine.
  • Plan their week of homework and activities.
  • Look after the pet almost on their own.

How to get started without a battle

  1. Just a few. Start with 2 or 3. If it goes well, add one more the following week.
  2. Clear and specific. “Tidy your room” is huge; “toys in the box” makes sense.
  3. Where they can see it. A sheet on the fridge, at their height. If the list only lives in your head, it’s your list, not theirs.
  4. Weeks with a start and an end. You can celebrate a week; a never-ending chart gets boring.
  5. Teamwork, not orders. Swap “you have to” for “would you be in charge of…?”.
